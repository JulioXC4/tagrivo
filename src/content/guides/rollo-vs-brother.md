Both printers can belong on a shipping desk, but the decision is more useful when framed around workflow than a single “best” label. This is a specification-based comparison, not a hands-on review.

## The core difference

[Rollo Wireless](/printers/rollo-wireless/) uses 203 dpi direct-thermal printing with Wi-Fi and a USB fallback. [Brother QL-1110NWB](/printers/brother-ql1110nwb/) offers 300 dpi, a cutter, and a broader set of interfaces including Ethernet.

Rollo accepts generic direct-thermal stock. Brother uses DK-format rolls. These differences affect stock purchasing, loading, and available workspace.

## When Rollo is worth examining

If your main concern is printing from a computer and phone over a shared Wi-Fi network, examine the Rollo setup instructions first. Its AirPrint certification is relevant to compatible Apple workflows.

Do not mistake Bluetooth-assisted setup for direct Bluetooth printing. Also leave room for externally fed media behind the printer. The standard Android mobile flow has a documented 4 × 6 restriction.

## When Brother is worth examining

Brother's 300 dpi specification may matter when you want finer printed detail. The automatic cutter and Ethernet connection are concrete differences that may suit a particular workstation.

They also introduce different considerations: price compatible DK rolls by usable label, verify the current OS support matrix, and check whether the listing is the NWB or another variant. The manufacturer's address-label speed claim is not a claim about full shipping-label throughput.

## Compare the task, not just the resolution

For a computer-and-phone setup, first confirm the original PDF and the application that prints it. Rollo's Wi-Fi route may fit, but that does not make direct printing from every seller app verified. Brother's additional interfaces also remain app-dependent. Our [mobile guide](/guides/iphone-android-shipping-labels/) follows those separate steps.

For a shared workstation that needs Ethernet or repeated cutting, Brother has documented features that Rollo lacks. Those features can justify investigating it, but do not establish a time saving. Decide who will load rolls, manage drivers and keep the network route working. The practical workflow has more components than the printhead.

For a fixed computer, check the OS and CPU before choosing either. Brother's Automatic Crop feature is documented for Windows; do not assume a Mac offers that same function. Rollo's Windows Arm driver restriction is another reason to use the [computer compatibility checklist](/guides/mac-windows-label-printer-compatibility/) before ordering.

## Put equivalent media quotes beside the hardware

Compare stock that produces the label you actually need. Rollo's generic media options and Brother's DK-format rolls should not receive an identical cost-per-label assumption merely to simplify a table. For continuous media, account for the usable label length and cutting. Include delivery in your quote if you want it represented.

The comparison tool lets you enter a separate hardware and media quote for each printer. Try the same order volume and waste rate for both, then vary an uncertain assumption. Its totals describe the modelled consumption; buying whole packs can require more cash upfront. Read the [cost guide](/guides/printing-costs/) for that distinction.

## What this comparison cannot establish

We have not measured scan reliability, setup time, long-term failure rates, or customer support responsiveness. Resolution alone cannot establish any of those outcomes.

Neither figure here is a live offer: the US manufacturer pages showed Rollo at $279.99 and Brother at $334.99 on October 29, 2026. Brother's page also marked the model out of stock at that check. Obtain current totals, including tax and delivery, and confirm availability before comparing ownership cost. Also check your processor: Rollo documents a Windows ARM driver restriction.
